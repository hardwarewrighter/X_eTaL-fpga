# Activation and gating functions on an FPGA

How X_eTaL's neural-network functions become circuits on the Tang
boards: what activation and gating functions do, the arithmetic that
makes them exact in hardware, how data gets from a laptop, an SD card or
a camera to the FPGA and back, how the work runs in parallel, what fits
on the Tang Nano 9K and the Tang Primer 20K, and a set of demos up to a
camera that identifies objects. A design note (2026-10-23): numbers
marked "to verify" have not been measured or checked with the tools
yet. The roadmap is in [plan.md](plan.md) (the fpga-nn work).

## What they are for

- **Activation functions** make a network more than one linear map:
  each neuron computes `W x + b` and then a nonlinear function of it.
  In hardware the activation runs **once per neuron**, after N
  multiply-accumulates (MACs), so it is never the expensive part.
  - By comparison and shift (a few LUTs): ReLU `max(x, 0)`, leaky ReLU
    (a shift for the negative side), ReLU6 and hard-tanh (clamps),
    hard-sigmoid and hard-swish (piecewise linear).
  - By table: sigmoid, tanh, SiLU (`x * sigmoid(x)`), GELU, and exp (for
    softmax). With 8-bit activations every possible input is one of
    256, so a 256-entry table is exact by construction, not an
    approximation of the quantized function. One 18 Kbit block RAM
    (2048 x 9) holds eight such tables.
- **Gating functions** let the network decide how much of a value
  passes: `y = v * sigmoid(g)` (GLU), `y = v * SiLU(g)` (SwiGLU), with
  `v` and `g` two projections of the same input.
  - LSTM and GRU cells gate what their memory keeps and forgets
    (sigmoid gates, tanh candidates).
  - GLU and SwiGLU are the gated feed-forward block of current
    transformers.
  - A mixture-of-experts router gates which sub-network runs (top-k of
    a softmax).
  - In hardware a gate is one table lookup and one multiply per neuron.

## Where the functions come from (moving)

X_eTaL itself and X_eTaL-libraries are refactoring and adding math,
activation and gating functions. The names used here (X_eTaL-ML's
`nn:s_igmoid`, `nn:t_anh`, `nn:r_elu`) may move into the base language
or a shared library, and SiLU, GELU and the gates may arrive there as
library functions. This repository takes them from wherever the pinned
commits (`XETAL_COMMIT`, `XETAL_LIBRARIES_COMMIT`) define them, moving
a pin only between steps as usual, and does not define its own copies
except as a named workaround in [xetal-asks.md](xetal-asks.md).

## The arithmetic

Integers only, the evaluator as the specification (plan A2, A3):

1. Weights and activations are 8-bit, accumulators 32-bit (`libs/Fixed`
   wraps, so the evaluator computes what the circuit does).
2. After a neuron's N MACs and its bias, a requantization brings the
   32-bit sum back to 8 bits: multiply by a fixed-point scale, shift,
   round, saturate. One scale per layer (or per output channel).
3. The 8-bit result indexes the activation table, or goes through the
   compare logic.
4. For a gate: `v` requantized, `g` through its table, the product
   requantized again.

The tables are generated by X_eTaL from the float functions (today
X_eTaL-ML's `nn:s_igmoid` and `nn:t_anh`, and SiLU and GELU from them)
applied to the 256 inputs and rounded once; the same table is the evaluator's integer
function and the circuit's block RAM, so the board's answers are
bit-exact with the evaluator's, as the HEX1000 calculator's are.

## Parallelism

- **Lanes (output-parallel):** the input `x_j` is broadcast to L MAC
  lanes, each computing a different output neuron from its own weight
  bank. A layer of M neurons and N inputs takes `ceil(M / L) * N`
  clocks. Each lane needs its own bank because a block RAM gives two
  reads a clock: L lanes need about L / 2 blocks of weights read in
  parallel, which is what sets L, as much as the multipliers do. This
  is the `--lanes N` schedule of plan A8.
- **Shared activations:** the L results arrive once every N clocks, so
  one dual-port table serves them all, 2 a clock, while the lanes
  accumulate the next L neurons. Replicating tables buys nothing.
- **Gated blocks:** the value and gate projections run as two lane
  groups at once; the gate is one multiply per neuron.
- **Layer pipelining:** with ping-pong buffers between layers, layer 2
  works on input k while layer 1 works on input k + 1. It raises the
  throughput of a stream (a camera, an SD card benchmark), not the
  latency of one input.
- **Convolutions:** line buffers hold the rows a 3 x 3 window needs;
  each weight is reused at every pixel, so weights stay on chip and the
  lanes run across output channels.

## Data paths in and out

### 1. Laptop over USB-UART (works today)

The path the 9K calculator already proves:

1. Before the bitstream: X_eTaL-ML trains or loads the model;
   X_eTaL quantizes it and writes weights, biases, scales and tables as
   block-RAM contents (`$readmemh`), baked into the bitstream.
2. Per inference, the laptop sends a frame: sync byte, command (run,
   select a function, load weights), length, payload, checksum.
3. The UART receiver fills an input buffer; the controller runs the
   layers (lanes, requantize, activate, next layer); argmax.
4. The UART returns the class and scores (and a cycle count); the
   laptop runs the same input through the evaluator and reports whether
   the two are identical.

The UART is the bottleneck: at 115200 baud (the rate tested on the 9K)
a 28 x 28 image takes about 68 ms to send and under 0.1 ms to classify.
Higher rates through the 9K's USB bridge are to verify. This path
suits the activation-unit demo, single queries, and collecting training
images from a camera (a 32 x 32 frame is 1 KB, about 10 frames a
second at 115200).

### 2. SD card over SPI (bypasses the UART)

Both boards have a TF (microSD) slot wired to the FPGA (the 9K's
pins to confirm from Sipeed's schematic). An SD card in SPI mode
needs four signals (clock, command in, data out, chip select) and a
small controller:

- Start-up: clock at 400 kHz or less, CMD0 (reset), CMD8 (voltage),
  ACMD41 until ready, CMD58 (block or byte addressing), then the full
  clock.
- Reads: CMD17 (one 512-byte block) or CMD18 (a stream of blocks until
  CMD12), each block a start token, 512 bytes and a CRC.
- At 13.5 MHz (27 MHz / 2) about 1.5 MB/s after overhead; up to 25 MHz
  in SPI mode, to verify with the card and the wiring. Four-bit SD mode
  is faster but much more logic; SPI mode is enough here.

No file system: the laptop writes a raw image to a **dedicated** card
(writing it erases the card), and the FPGA reads blocks by number:

| Blocks | Contents |
| ------ | -------- |
| 0 | header: magic, version, counts and starting blocks of each part, checksums |
| next | the model: weights, biases, scales, tables (loaded into block RAM, or DDR3 on the 20K, at start) |
| next | the test set: inputs and labels, packed back to back |

What it buys:

- **On-board benchmark:** the FPGA streams the whole MNIST test set
  (10,000 images of 784 bytes and a label, about 7.9 MB) from the card,
  classifies each and counts the matches; only the summary (accuracy,
  cycles, the first mismatching indexes) goes over the UART. About 5 s
  for the set, limited by the card, not the network. The evaluator runs
  the same quantized model on the same set, so the two accuracies and
  mismatch lists must be identical, image for image.
- **Models without rebuilding:** a different card loads a different
  model at start, with no new bitstream (within the same layer shapes).
- **Larger models on the 20K:** weights loaded from the card into DDR3.

It does not help a camera demo (the pixels come from the camera), and
it is a model-and-data store, not a fast link to the laptop.

### 3. A camera straight into the FPGA (no laptop in the loop)

- **Tang Nano 9K:** no camera connector (Sipeed lists a camera only for
  the Nano 4K, an OV2640), but a DVP camera module wires to its
  headers: an OV7670 (no FIFO) or OV2640 breakout needs D0 to D7, PCLK,
  HREF, VSYNC, XCLK (from the FPGA), the SCCB pair (SIOC, SIOD), RESET
  and PWDN, about 14 pins, plus 3.3 V and ground. Free header pins to
  confirm with the board lane's pin guard.
- **Tang Primer 20K:** the Dock has a 24-pin DVP FPC connector (the
  sensor Sipeed pairs with it, and its open-flow use, to verify).

The circuit, all at the camera's pixel rate:

1. An SCCB writer sets the sensor up (a ROM of register writes):
   QVGA, YUV422, a fixed exposure if the lighting allows.
2. Capture keeps Y (gray) and drops U and V.
3. A box filter shrinks the image on the fly (a 4 x 4 or 5 x 5 average,
   one line of partial sums) to 64 x 48, cropped to 32 x 32 or 48 x 48:
   no full frame is stored.
4. The small frame goes into one half of a ping-pong buffer (1 to 2 KB);
   the classifier runs on the other half while the next frame arrives.
5. The class goes to the LEDs, the UART, and on boards with HDMI an
   overlay: the small frame scaled up with the label (Apicula has a DVI
   example; the Primer 20K Dock's HDMI and its open-flow use to verify).

## What fits

| | Tang Nano 9K | Tang Primer 20K |
| - | - | - |
| Logic | 8,640 LUT4, 6,480 flip-flops | 20,736 LUT4, 15,552 flip-flops |
| Block RAM | 26 x 18 Kbit (about 52 KB at 8 bits) | 46 x 18 Kbit (about 92 KB) |
| Hardware multipliers | 20 (18 x 18) | 48 (18 x 18) |
| Large memory | 8 MB PSRAM | 128 MB DDR3 |
| Camera | DVP module on the headers | DVP FPC on the Dock |
| SD card | TF slot | TF slot |
| Practical 8-bit MAC lanes | about 20 to 40 | about 48 to 96 |
| Weights on chip | about 45 KB | about 85 KB |

The lane counts assume each 18 x 18 multiplier does one 8 x 8 MAC, or
two in its 9 x 9 mode, plus some LUT multipliers (60 to 80 LUTs each);
the multiplier counts and Apicula's support of the 9 x 9 mode on both
families are to verify.

Examples within the 9K's on-chip weights: a 784 to 56 to 10 digit
classifier (about 44 KB), a SwiGLU block with width 64 and hidden 128
(3 x 64 x 128, about 25 KB), a GRU with 64 hidden units over 64 inputs
(about 25 KB), a small camera CNN (below, about 6 KB). The 20K holds
about twice as much on chip.

**DDR3 is for data, not streamed weights.** 96 lanes at 50 MHz consume
about 5 GB/s of weights if none is reused; the 20K's 16-bit DDR3 gives
well under 1 GB/s in practice. Weights that fit on chip stay there;
DDR3 holds frames, activations of large layers, test sets loaded from
the card, and models swapped in between runs. An open-flow DDR3
controller for the GW2A (community ones exist) is to verify; the
Tang Nano 20K, with SDRAM in the package, is the simpler fallback when
a camera can come in another way.

### The camera classifier in numbers

On the 9K, at 32 x 32 gray: 3 x 3 convolutions of 8, 16 and 32
channels, each followed by ReLU and a 2 x 2 max pool, a global average
and a dense layer to k classes.

- Weights: about 6 K (72 + 1,152 + 4,608 + 32 k), all on chip.
- Work: about 0.7 million MACs a frame; at 32 lanes and 27 MHz about
  0.8 ms, far below a camera's 33 ms frame time even at a fraction of
  full lane use.
- Buffers: the input frame (1 KB), the first layer's output (32 x 32 x
  8, 8 KB) and smaller ones after, in ping-pong block RAM.

The 20K's extra lanes and memory go to a larger input (64 x 64 or
96 x 96), more channels and more classes, and DDR3 frame buffers for a
full-resolution HDMI view.

What is realistic: **classification** of a handful of classes (5 to
10: hand gestures, a few known objects, playing cards, person or no
person) with a controlled background and lighting, trained on images
taken with the same camera (collected through the board itself over the
UART). What is not: detection of many object kinds with boxes around
them (YOLO-style), which needs far more weights and MACs than these
boards have.

## Order of work

1. The neuron with ReLU at 8 bits, then the activation and gate tables
   (demo 1), on the 9K over the UART.
2. The lane schedule and weight banks (demo 2 over the UART), then the
   SD card reader and the on-board benchmark.
3. The camera on the 9K: SCCB, capture, shrink, then the classifier
   (demo 5), with the training images collected through the board.
4. Gated recurrence and SwiGLU (demos 3 and 4).
5. The 20K: its flow, DDR3, the Dock camera; the larger camera model.

Each step keeps the rule of the calculator: the X_eTaL program first,
its evaluator's answers pinned, and the circuit and the board
reproducing them exactly.
